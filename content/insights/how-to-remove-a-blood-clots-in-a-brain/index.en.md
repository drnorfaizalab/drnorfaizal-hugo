It often begins with what patients describe as "the worst headache of my life." A sudden, explosive pain that signals something is catastrophically wrong. This is the moment a blood vessel in the brain may have burst, causing a bleed known as an intracerebral hemorrhage.

As blood pools, it forms a clot, or hematoma. Our skull is a rigid, enclosed space, and this growing clot has nowhere to go. It begins to press on the delicate structures of the brain, disrupting function and, if left untreated, threatening life. In these moments, every single minute matters.

For many years, the standard approach was a large open surgery called a craniotomy. While life-saving, it involved a significant incision and a long, difficult recovery. Today, we have a more precise, less traumatic option: minimally invasive hematoma evacuation.

Using either an endoscope (a tiny camera) or a specialised cannula guided by advanced imaging, we can navigate directly to the hematoma. Through an incision often just a few centimetres long, we can gently aspirate, or suction out, the clot. This immediately relieves the dangerous pressure on the brain.

What does this mean for the patient? It means less trauma to healthy brain tissue. It means a smaller scar, a shorter stay in the intensive care unit, and a faster start to the rehabilitation that is so crucial for recovery. It represents a shift from a procedure of necessity to one of elegance and precision.

But technology is only one part of the equation. The other, more critical part, is time. The best outcomes happen when we can intervene early, and that relies on you—patients, family members, and fellow doctors—recognising the warning signs.

This is not a 'wait and see' situation. If you or someone you love experiences these symptoms, it is a medical emergency:

*   A sudden, severe headache, unlike any you've had before.
*   Sudden weakness, numbness, or paralysis, especially on one side of the body.
*   Slurred speech or difficulty finding words.
*   A sudden decline in consciousness or confusion, particularly after a head injury.

Recognising these red flags and seeking immediate emergency care is the first and most critical step. It’s what gives us the window of opportunity to use these advanced techniques to not just save a life, but to preserve its quality.

## Frequently asked questions

**What causes a blood clot to form in the brain?**
A blood clot, or hematoma, in the brain is most often caused by a hemorrhagic stroke, where a blood vessel ruptures. It can also result from a severe head injury, a ruptured aneurysm, or an arteriovenous malformation (AVM). High blood pressure is a major risk factor for these events.

**What are the main symptoms of a brain hemorrhage?**
The symptoms are often sudden and severe. Key warning signs include an abrupt, intense headache (often called a 'thunderclap headache'), weakness or numbness on one side of the body, slurred speech or difficulty understanding others, and a rapid decline in consciousness.

**Is minimally invasive surgery for a brain blood clot safe?**
Yes, for suitable candidates, it is a very safe and effective approach. Compared to traditional open surgery (craniotomy), minimally invasive techniques involve smaller incisions, less trauma to surrounding brain tissue, and typically lead to lower risks of infection and faster recovery times.

**How long is the recovery after minimally invasive brain clot removal?**
Recovery varies for each patient, but it is generally much faster than after open surgery. Patients often spend less time in the ICU and hospital overall. The focus of recovery is on rehabilitation therapy to regain any lost function, such as speech or motor skills.
