*After emergency surgery saved his life from a ruptured brain blood vessel, a father lived for six months missing part of his skull. His wife stepped up as head of the household while fighting fear and exhaustion, until a custom titanium plate from Korea was finally ready to close the defect.*

Six months ago, a family's life changed in an instant. A father was rushed to the hospital after an abnormal tangle of blood vessels in his brain, known as a cerebral arteriovenous malformation or AVM, suddenly ruptured. The bleeding caused the pressure inside his skull to spike to life-threatening levels.

To save his life, I had to perform an emergency decompressive craniectomy. A section of his skull bone was removed to give his swelling brain room to expand. He survived, but the real journey had only just begun. He left the hospital with a visible indentation on the side of his head, a fragile state that required constant care while waiting for his brain to recover.

Once his condition stabilised, the next hurdle appeared. We had to treat the root cause of the bleed, the AVM still sitting inside his brain. Laid out before the family were three treatment paths: open surgery, radiation, or embolisation. Each option carried frightening potential complications. For a family that had just come close to losing their loved one, the fear of what could go wrong made every decision feel overwhelming.

During those months, the heaviest weight fell on his wife. The husband who used to anchor the household now needed help with basic movement and clear thinking. With no time to hesitate, she stepped into the role of head of the family. She had to keep working to support them, care for their children, tend to her recovering husband, and coordinate every hospital admission and follow-up appointment.

The relentless pressure of balancing these roles slowly wore her patience thin. Every time she looked at the sunken defect on her husband's head, anxiety and fatigue crept in. Instinctively, she wanted everything fixed quickly. She wanted his skull closed right away so their lives could return to normal.

Yet in neurosurgery, rushing invites danger. Before we can close a skull defect through a procedure called cranioplasty, the underlying AVM must be secured first. Closing the skull too early would limit our options and create higher risks if an emergency arose during treatment.

I sat down with them to steady their nerves and reset expectations. Instead of viewing the waiting period as endless suffering, I suggested that she manage her husband's recovery like a project management timeline. Every phase had a distinct purpose and sequence. We could not skip to the final step before the foundation was safe.

She took a deep breath, let go of the urge for an immediate fix, and committed to following the plan one milestone at a time.

The next phase began with AVM embolisation alongside our interventional radiology team. It was not a single-session fix. He had to undergo two staged cycles of treatment. One month ago, the second and final cycle of embolisation was completed.

The shift that followed became the turning point that lifted the entire family. Freed from the abnormal blood flow that had been stealing resources from his brain, his cognition sharpened noticeably. The strength in his arms and legs improved significantly. Watching her husband speak clearly again and move with steadier power made every sleepless night worth enduring for his wife.

Now, the final phase has arrived. Using detailed scans of his head, a custom-made titanium plate was ordered from Korea, shaped precisely to match his missing skull bone. Cranioplasty is scheduled to close the defect, restore physical protection to his brain, and bring back his natural head contour.

Seeing his wife in the clinic as we planned this final surgery, I thought back to the anxious, exhausted face I saw six months earlier. She was no longer rushed or ruled by fear. She was composed, organised, and steady, having seen each step through to completion.

Brain healing never happens overnight. It demands extraordinary patience, especially from the family members carrying the load behind the scenes. When we learn to trust a structured medical process and give the body the time each phase requires, the outcome we are waiting for arrives right on schedule.

## What It Means For You

Treating a ruptured cerebral AVM and an associated skull defect requires a staged approach to keep the patient safe. After a life-saving decompressive craniectomy, the brain needs time for swelling to settle, and the underlying AVM must be treated before the skull is reconstructed. Cranioplasty using a custom-fitted titanium plate is only performed once the brain is stable and the vascular risk is controlled. Following a clear treatment timeline reduces complications and supports better neurological recovery.
